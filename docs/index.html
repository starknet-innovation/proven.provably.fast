Help make STARK proofs smaller

Find the mathematics that lets a STARK make fewer checks without losing security. This challenge targets a missing soundness bound; concrete proof-size savings require a complete protocol analysis.

The challenge: bound how often a random combination can hide a bad input, then turn the bound into prover parameters. That count is the number of bad challenges. Counterexamples exist in important binary-field settings; sharper bounds on the power-of-two domains prime-field STARKs use remain open.

Its count tasks are an open RL environment: experimental Harbor tasks with certificate-checked scores, graded in a separate verifier environment, and every find is credited to the person or agent that made it. Proofs are open research, accepted after independent review.

Start here

The first goal: at most 16 n bad challenges. Prove this bound at exact code rate 1/2 and agreement at least 5/8, on every domain covered by the pinned statement, for all n above an explicit threshold N. The statement is open. An application at n = 220 also needs N ≤ 220 and a complete soundness analysis for the chosen protocol. This is an open count bound: any claim of fewer queries must establish applicability at the intended length and account for the complete protocol error budget.

The exact quantifiers

Give an explicit N. For every n ≥ N with k = n/2, every agreement threshold A satisfying 5n ≤ 8A ≤ 8n, every field of characteristic zero or p > k − 1, and every multiplicative coset of a subgroup of order n = 2m in that field, prove the pinned bad-challenge count is at most 16 n. The quantifiers include words taking values in the challenge field. The Lean definition (lean/PINNED.md) is authoritative.

Starter kit: kit/, two scripts that build a line on a BabyBear or KoalaBear subgroup and certify its bad challenges.

  1. 1

    Prove a bound

    Improve an upper bound in a stated regime. Give the field, domain, agreement threshold, constants and range of n. Explain any reduction from list size to the bad-challenge count. T3 thread.

  2. 2

    Find a counterexample

    Use the starter kit to construct a line and certify distinct bad challenges. Compare it with the baseline at the same parameters, and publish the instance, its certificate and its hash. A finite example above 16 n disproves that finite instance of the proposed bound. To refute the existence of any threshold N, construct counterexamples above 16 n at unbounded admissible lengths.

    This task also runs as an RL environment: experimental Harbor tasks with certificate-checked scores. For the count tasks, reward = min(1, certified distinct challenges / (16 n)); the uncapped count and the baseline comparison are reported separately. A score does not establish novelty or solve a target.

  3. 3

    Review a claim

    Check a proof, reproduce a computation or formalize a result in Lean. State exactly what you checked and what remains conditional. Reviews and reproductions receive credit.

How a theorem gets made

Share a claim with its assumptions and evidence. Other participants test and review it. The record preserves the contribution, its dependencies and the scope of each review.

A target is solved only when an independently reviewed proof meets its pinned statement. Lean verification is recorded separately.

What is known, and what is open

In plain words. A verifier mixes two committed words with a random number z and checks only the mix. A bad challenge makes the combined data look close to a codeword without a consistent codeword explanation for both inputs on the same positions. Count how many bad z a cheating prover can have. The precise version:

Take a Reed‑Solomon code of rate ρ on n prescribed points over a field of large characteristic. For words f and g and a polynomial P of degree below k, let S be the full set of evaluation points where f + zg equals P. A challenge z is bad if some P has |S| ≥ A, but there are no polynomials Q and R of degree below k that agree with f and g, respectively, throughout S. Count distinct z, not witnesses.

  • Agreement above √ρ, the Johnson thresholdLinear knownBCIKS20; Dao, Kominers and Thaler 2026, Theorem 5.12, in every characteristic
  • Between the first-order curve and √ρQuadratic known, T1 openDao, Kominers and Thaler 2026. A workshop construction gives the lower constant 78/25 = 3.12 for the domain-uniform T1Uniform statement at slack 6/25; it does not give that constant for the half-rate subgroup goal
  • Just below the first-order curveCubic known, T2 openTwo hidden derivatives: Dao, Kominers and Thaler 2026, Theorems 6.3 and 6.6
  • A fixed gap δ above the rateT3 openKnown exponents in the thousands, 27 at best at δ = 0.2. Lower bound linear with a constant near 21/δ, found here. Linear for folded and subspace-design codes (Goyal and Guruswami 2025)
  • Counterexamples in important binary-field settingsBackgroundDao, Kominers, Thaler and Zheng construct counterexamples on additive domains relevant to binary-field SNARKs, including examples close to Johnson. Their results do not settle the large-characteristic targets here

Dao, Kominers and Thaler report an 11.1 percent reduction for the ProveKit passport proof and a 4.63 percent reduction for the final compressed ZisK proof in their worked configurations; those are results of that paper, and the workshop has not established a new protocol reduction.
